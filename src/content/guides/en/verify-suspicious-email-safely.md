## Suspicious email from a bank, Google, or courier: a safe verification guide

Check suspicious messages independently and respond quickly if credentials or card data were exposed.

> **Important**: Service interfaces and policies can change. Confirm current steps in official documentation.

## Step-by-step solution

### 1. Pause before clicking

Urgent threats, surprise invoices, and immediate payment demands warrant independent verification. Do not open attachments or follow email links until you check the request elsewhere.

### 2. Inspect the sender with care

Display names can be forged. Review the full sender address and domain, but remember that even a familiar domain or existing thread is not a guarantee. A logo proves nothing.

### 3. Open the service independently

Use the official app or manually enter the known website address. Check whether the claimed payment, shipment, or security alert appears there. Contact banks through their official app or the number printed on your card.

### 4. Treat attachments cautiously

Do not run executable files, enable macros, or follow instructions to disable protection. Verify unexpected documents through a separate channel. Antivirus scanning helps but is not conclusive.

### 5. If you entered a password

Visit the real service independently, change the password, revoke unfamiliar sessions, review recovery options, and enable multi-factor authentication. Alert IT for work accounts.

### 6. If you exposed card details or sent money

Contact the bank immediately through an official channel to protect the card and ask about dispute options. Preserve evidence. Beware of recovery scammers demanding upfront fees.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] HTTPS does not prove a site is trustworthy.
- [ ] Sender addresses are clues, not conclusive proof.
- [ ] Rapid action matters after account compromise.
